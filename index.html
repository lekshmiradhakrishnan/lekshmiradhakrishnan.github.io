Lekshmi Radhakrishnan

Research Assistant, Cultural Dynamics Lab
ALM candidate in Psychology, Harvard University

Many people never seek therapy because they expect to spend it explaining themselves rather than being helped.

I'm interested in how the interpretive frames people inherit — transmitted across generations rather than chosen — shape the way psychological distress is recognized and treated. My focus is classification accuracy: cultural distance between clinician and patient can produce error in both directions, pathologizing what is culturally ordinary and missing genuine illness expressed in unfamiliar terms. The pattern is well documented in psychotic-spectrum illness, where diagnostic and treatment disparities across racial and ethnic groups are established, and that is also where my clinical grounding lies.

That question isn't abstract for me. I grew up between Kerala and the United States, and I have spent most of my life watching people in one culture fail to make sense of perspectives that another treats as obvious. In clinical work, that gap has consequences: people decide the explaining isn't worth it, and quietly go without care.

My personal questions and clinical experience found a framework while I was working with Dr. Mona Weissmark on Seeing the Other Side: Shifting Perceptions, on the psychology of perspective-taking across group and cultural lines. It gave me a way to think about what it takes to see from another position, and introduced me to research on transgenerational trauma and on why certain frames are so hard for people to step outside.

Clinical background

For two years I managed a residential facility run by the Mental Health Association of South Central Kansas, home to more than 30 adults with chronic psychiatric conditions, most on the psychotic spectrum. A substantial number of residents were immigrants, and many spoke little or no English. It is the reason I think about assessment the way I do: I have watched what happens when a clinician and a patient do not share a frame for what is being described.

Languages

I speak English, Malayalam, Tamil, and Hindi, and can recruit, interview, and run fieldwork in South Asian and diaspora communities without an interpreter.

My training so far spans four levels of the problem: culture, clinical phenomena, structure, and biology. A person who is misread in a clinic, or who never reaches one, is being acted on at all four at once, and I don't think you can fix the problem from any one level alone.

Elsewhere

I have lived in India and across the United States, and traveled to twenty-eight countries on five continents, most of them alone, on one-way tickets with no route planned — where I went next decided by the next cheapest fare and the people I met along the way.
