Many people never try therapy because they expect to spend it explaining themselves rather than being helped.

I study how cultural context shapes the way psychological distress is expressed, understood, and treated — and why treatments developed in one cultural setting so often fail to reach people from another.

That question isn't abstract for me. I grew up between Kerala and the United States, and I have spent most of my life watching people in one culture fail to make sense of perspectives that another treats as obvious. In clinical work, that gap has consequences: people decide the explaining isn't worth it, and quietly go without care.

My thinking here was shaped early by working with Mona Weissmark on Seeing the Other Side: Shifting Perceptions, on the psychology of perspective-taking across group and cultural lines. It gave me two things I still use: a serious framework for what it actually takes to see from another position, and transgenerational trauma as a way of understanding why some frames are so hard to step outside of.

Culture, clinical phenomena, structure, biology. A person avoiding a therapist's office is being acted on at all four levels at once, and I don't think you can fix the problem from only one of them.

A study of my own

Can learning about bonobo female leadership reduce gender stereotypes in humans?

Claims that male dominance is biologically mandated are remarkably durable, and they draw their authority from a selective reading of our closest relatives. Bonobos — as close to us as chimpanzees are — organise very differently.

I designed a study asking whether brief exposure to material on bonobo female leadership and coalition-building produces a measurable shift in gender-stereotype attitudes, assessed by Implicit Association Test and open-ended reflection. The aim is narrative change through the path of least resistance: not argument, but a wider set of facts about what evolution actually permits.

It began as a proposal for a graduate proseminar and I have kept working on it since, with input from faculty in primatology, evolutionary neuroscience, and affective neuroscience.

Before research

Team Leader, psychiatric assisted living facilityMental Health Association of South Central Kansas. I managed a residential facility for 30+ adults with chronic psychiatric conditions, most on the psychotic spectrum, including paranoid schizophrenia — conducting functional assessments and intake reviews, designing individualised care plans, delivering crisis intervention, and training staff. A substantial number of those residents were immigrants, and many spoke little or no English. It is the reason I think about assessment the way I do: I have watched what happens when a clinician and a patient do not share a frame for what is being described.
