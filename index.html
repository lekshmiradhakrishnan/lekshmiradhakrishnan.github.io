Lekshmi Radhakrishnan

Research Assistant, Cultural Dynamics Lab
ALM candidate in Psychology, Harvard University

Many people never try therapy because they expect to spend it explaining themselves rather than being helped.

I study how cultural context shapes the way psychological distress is recognized and treated. Culture here means the interpretive frames people inherit — transmitted across generations rather than chosen — and my interest is in classification accuracy: cultural distance between clinician and patient produces error in both directions, pathologising what is culturally ordinary and missing genuine illness expressed in unfamiliar terms.

That question isn't abstract for me. I grew up between Kerala and the United States, and I have spent most of my life watching people in one culture fail to make sense of perspectives that another treats as obvious. In clinical work, that gap has consequences: people decide the explaining isn't worth it, and quietly go without care.

Some of this goes back to working with Mona Weissmark on Seeing the Other Side: Shifting Perceptions, on the psychology of perspective-taking across group and cultural lines. It gave me a working framework for what it takes to see from another position, and introduced me to research on transgenerational trauma and on why certain frames are so hard for people to step outside.

Independent research

Does evidence about a closely related species change what people take to be biologically fixed?

Beliefs about what human nature requires are remarkably durable, and they often draw their authority from a selective reading of our closest relatives. Bonobos are as closely related to us as chimpanzees are, and they organise very differently — which makes them a useful test of whether beliefs of that kind track the evidence at all.

I designed a study asking whether brief exposure to material on bonobo social organisation, including female coalitions and leadership, produces a measurable shift in beliefs about sex differences, assessed by Implicit Association Test and open-ended reflection. The underlying question is about belief revision: whether a claim people treat as settled by biology moves when the biological evidence turns out to be more varied than assumed.

Since drafting it I have consulted faculty in primatology, evolutionary neuroscience, and affective neuroscience on the design.

The obvious objection is that brief interventions on implicit measures rarely hold. Lai and colleagues (2016) tested nine of them across 6,321 participants: all nine reduced implicit racial preference immediately, none survived a delay of hours to days. I treat that as the design constraint rather than a reason not to run it, which is why the protocol pairs the implicit measure with open-ended reflection, and why the question worth asking is whether a belief about what evolution permits behaves differently from an evaluative association.

Clinical and community work

Team Leader, psychiatric assisted living facilityMental Health Association of South Central Kansas. I managed a residential facility for 30+ adults with chronic psychiatric conditions, most on the psychotic spectrum, including paranoid schizophrenia — conducting functional assessments and intake reviews, designing individualised care plans, delivering crisis intervention, and training staff. A substantial number of those residents were immigrants, and many spoke little or no English. It is the reason I think about assessment the way I do: I have watched what happens when a clinician and a patient do not share a frame for what is being described.
